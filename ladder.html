Case 3

The prompt ladder, including the rung that broke

The problem

I was using AI badly and getting away with it. My prompts worked well enough that I never learned which part of them was doing the work, which meant I could not repeat a good result on purpose and could not fix a bad one except by rewriting the whole thing.

The task I tested it on is real and it is the same task as case 1: given a list of columns and a label definition, decide which columns are safe to use as features.

What I did

Six runs. One weak baseline, then five versions, each adding exactly one layer, each attacking whatever was worst about the previous output. Every output saved and compared side by side rather than from memory.

The rule that made it worth doing was one change per version. Change two things and an improvement tells you nothing about which one caused it.

The version that broke

V4 is the reason this case is on the site.

I gave it two example rows from a good version of the same table on a different dataset, expecting it to raise the quality of the reasoning. Instead it copied the reasoning. Five of eight columns came back with the identical phrase from my examples, "fixed at collection time, cannot change after," including one column that V3 had correctly flagged as a problem.

Worse, my two examples happened to use only two verdicts, safe and unsafe. The conditional category disappeared from the output entirely, because nothing in my examples demonstrated it. And because my examples came from a dataset where the danger was a single column, it went looking for a single column here, marked one half of the leaking pair unsafe and the other half safe, and missed the actual risk.

Examples do not just show quality. They narrow the search, and they silently delete any category they do not contain. On a task where I already know the shape of the answer that is fine. On a task where I am hoping to be told something I missed, it is the wrong tool.

I kept V4 in the ladder. A ladder where all five rungs improve is a ladder that was not tested hard enough.

What actually earned its place

Context. V2 was the only step that changed the answer's category rather than its degree. Once it had the real column names, the real label definition, and the three quirks in the data, it found the numerator and denominator pair on its own. If I could keep one layer this is it, and it is not close.

Verification. The layer I nearly skipped because it looked like politeness. Asking the model to argue against its own "safe" verdicts produced a backfill scenario that is a real failure mode in our warehouse, and it self-corrected the exact cell V3 had got wrong. Asking a model to attack its own answer is not politeness. It was the only step that turned a wrong cell into a right one.

What came of it

A prompt someone else can run without me in the room. I tested that claim on a dataset the prompt was not designed around, a support-ticket table with a "resolved within SLA" label. It found the created-at and resolved-at pair without being told to look for a pair, and flagged the assignee column as conditional because reassignment can happen after the outcome. Both correct.

The final version drops the examples layer. Four of five layers made it into the keeper, and knowing which one to leave out is most of what the exercise bought me.
